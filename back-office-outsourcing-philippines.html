Back Office Outsourcing in the Philippines

Back office outsourcing in the Philippines is about processing work done accurately, on time and inside your own platforms: claims, applications, loan files, order entry, reconciliations and document indexing. Since 2011, ProVizion Outsourcing has built dedicated processing teams for businesses in the US, Australia, the UK and Canada, recruited from a network of more than 10,000 Filipino professionals with backgrounds in insurance, lending, finance and operations.

Organisations including Allstate, Acrisure and Symmetry Financial Group have used Philippine processing capacity because it combines careful, detail-oriented staff with the ability to add capacity quickly when volume moves. This page covers the kinds of work that suit a processing team, how accuracy and turnaround are measured, the QA structures we use, how procedures are documented, and how the team absorbs month-end and renewal-season spikes.

Processing work that suits back office outsourcing in the Philippines

Back office work has a recognisable shape: a document or record arrives, it is checked, keyed, matched or routed according to rules, and it leaves with a status. Work of that shape transfers well because the rules can be written down and the output can be measured. Typical queues include:

  • Insurance claims intake, verification and status updates, expanded on our claims processing outsourcing page.
  • Policy, membership and account applications, including document checks and follow-up for missing items.
  • Loan and mortgage file preparation, conditions tracking and lender submissions.
  • Order entry, purchase order matching and fulfilment exceptions.
  • Bank, ledger and intercompany reconciliations with exception reporting.
  • Document indexing, classification and metadata capture for archives and case files.

Accuracy and turnaround: the two numbers that matter

Every processing engagement is built around two service levels. The first is accuracy, defined as the share of items that pass review without correction. The second is turnaround, defined as the elapsed time from an item entering the queue to it leaving with a final status. We agree both with you before go-live, along with how items are counted and what counts as an error, because vague definitions produce arguments rather than improvement.

Both figures are reported at whatever cadence you prefer, usually daily during ramp-up and weekly once stable. Where an item type is consistently missing target, the fix is usually a procedure gap or an unclear rule, and the report is what makes it visible.

QA sampling and maker-checker controls

Accuracy does not happen because people try hard; it happens because the process catches errors before they leave. Two controls do most of the work. For high-volume, lower-risk items we use statistical QA sampling: a QA analyst reviews a defined share of completed items each day, scores them against a checklist and feeds errors back to the individual processor for coaching. Sample sizes rise for new staff and new item types, then fall as results stabilise.

For higher-risk items, such as payments, policy changes or anything that is hard to reverse, we use maker-checker. One person prepares the item, a second person independently verifies it before it is released, and both names are recorded. Your own approvers can sit in the checker seat for the most sensitive categories if you prefer.

Turning your tribal knowledge into written procedures

Most businesses discover during onboarding that half their process lives in the heads of two long-serving staff. Part of our set-up work is capturing that into standard operating procedures: step-by-step instructions with screenshots, decision rules for the common exceptions and a clear "stop and ask" list for anything outside scope. The SOPs are stored in your document system and versioned, so they are yours to keep and update.

Once written, the SOPs become the training material for every new team member and the reference for QA scoring. They also make it far easier to add a second queue later, or to hand work to the wider offshore admin staff who support your business outside pure processing.

Handling month end, renewal season and other spikes

Processing volume is rarely flat. Finance teams see it at month end and year end, insurers see it during renewal season, lenders see it when the market shifts, and retailers see it before holidays. A dedicated team is sized for the normal load, and we plan for the peaks in three ways: cross-training team members across queues so people can shift to where the backlog is, agreeing overtime and extended-shift arrangements in advance, and keeping a bench of screened candidates who can be added on short notice for a defined period.

For clients whose peaks run to dozens of extra seats, we deliver through partner facilities of 1,000-plus seats, using the same SOPs and QA structure, so the surge team works to the same standard as the core team.

Frequently asked questions

What types of work are a poor fit for back office outsourcing in the Philippines?

Work that has no written rules and depends on one person's judgement, or that changes so often the procedure cannot keep up. If you can describe how an item is processed and what a correct result looks like, it usually transfers well. If not, we would suggest documenting it first.

How is an error defined when you measure accuracy?

We agree a written error taxonomy with you before go-live, distinguishing critical errors that affect a customer or a payment from minor ones such as formatting. Only items that fail review are counted, and each is attributed to a root cause so the fix goes to the right place.

Can our own staff review items before they are released?

Yes. Many clients keep an internal approver as the checker for high-value or irreversible items, at least during the first months. As confidence grows, that role can move to a ProVizion QA analyst or remain with you permanently. It is your decision.

How quickly can extra processors be added for a seasonal peak?

For a few additional seats, typically within days, drawing on our screened bench and cross-trained existing staff. For a much larger surge we plan several weeks ahead so training and access provisioning are complete before the volume arrives.

Who owns the standard operating procedures written during onboarding?

You do. They are written in your document system, under your accounts, and remain there regardless of how the engagement develops. We maintain them with you as processes change so they never drift out of date.
